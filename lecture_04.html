Introduction

Why decompose a time series?

A time series can combine several visual structures:

  • long-run movement;
  • seasonal repetition;
  • cycles;
  • irregular fluctuations;
  • calendar and scale effects.

Decomposition separates these layers so they can be inspected individually.

Decomposition is a visual and analytical device. It supports understanding and may also improve forecasting by isolating structures that require different treatment.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/03-decomposition.html

Three conceptual components

We usually combine trend and cycle into a trend-cycle component:

\[ y_t = \text{trend-cycle} + \text{seasonal} + \text{remainder}. \]

For high-frequency series, more than one seasonal component may be present.

The remainder is not automatically white noise. It is what remains after the selected decomposition procedure and parameter settings.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/03-decomposition.html

Learning objectives

By the end of the lecture, students will be able to:

  • distinguish transformations from decomposition;
  • explain additive and multiplicative structures;
  • calculate and visualize moving averages;
  • construct classical additive and multiplicative decompositions;
  • explain seasonal adjustment;
  • identify limitations of classical decomposition;
  • apply and tune STL;
  • diagnose leakage, outliers, and unstable seasonality;
  • communicate components without misleading scales.

The emphasis is visual reasoning supported by mathematically precise definitions and executable Python.

Analytical sequence

RAW SERIES
    ↓
KNOWN ADJUSTMENTS
    ↓
TRANSFORMATION / SCALE CHOICE
    ↓
TREND-CYCLE EXTRACTION
    ↓
SEASONAL EXTRACTION
    ↓
REMAINDER DIAGNOSTICS
    ↓
SEASONALLY ADJUSTED SERIES

Each arrow represents a modeling choice. Different choices can produce different component estimates.

Transformations and Adjustments

Why adjust before decomposing?

Adjustments can remove known or interpretable sources of variation:

  • calendar composition;
  • population growth;
  • inflation;
  • changing variance with the level.

The goal is a simpler, more stable structure.

An adjustment is justified by measurement and domain knowledge. It is not merely a cosmetic operation.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/03-decomposition.html

Inflation adjustment

Let \(y_t\) be a nominal value and \(z_t\) a price index. In base-period prices:

\[ x_t = \frac{y_t}{z_t}z_{\text{base}}. \]

If the index equals 100 in the base period:

\[ x_t = y_t\frac{100}{z_t}. \]

The adjusted series measures values in constant purchasing-power units. The price index, base period, and currency must be reported.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/03-decomposition.html

Nominal versus real visualization

Mathematical transformation

When variation grows with the level, a log transformation may stabilize relative variation:

\[ w_t = \log(y_t), \qquad y_t>0. \]

A change on the log scale corresponds to a relative change on the original scale:

\[ \log(y_t)-\log(y_{t-1}) = \log\left(\frac{y_t}{y_{t-1}}\right). \]

Natural logarithms are used throughout the Box-Cox discussion. Logs require strictly positive observations.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/03-decomposition.html

Original scale versus log scale

Modified Box-Cox family

The chapter uses a modified Box-Cox transformation:

\[ w_t= \begin{cases} \log(y_t), & \lambda=0,\\[4pt] \dfrac{\operatorname{sign}(y_t)|y_t|^\lambda-1}{\lambda}, & \lambda\ne0. \end{cases} \]

For the standard positive-data Box-Cox transformation, \(y_t>0\) is required.

At lambda equal to one, the transformed series is shifted by one without changing shape. Other lambda values change the shape and scale.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/03-decomposition.html

Visual effect of lambda

lambdas = [1.0, 0.5, 0.0, -0.5]

    ax.plot(positive_gas["ds"], transformed, color="#0072B2", linewidth=1)
    ax.set_title(f"λ = {lam:g}")
fig.suptitle("Box-Cox transformations of Australian gas production")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

This code uses SciPy’s standard positive-data Box-Cox implementation. The selection goal is a transformation that makes seasonal variation and variance more stable.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/03-decomposition.html

Data-driven lambda: document the criterion

lambda_mle = stats.boxcox_normmax(positive_gas["Gas"], method="mle")

np.float64(0.4374315661751587)

This is a maximum-likelihood choice from SciPy, not the Guerrero criterion used in the chapter’s boxcox_lambda() example.

Different selection criteria estimate lambda for different goals. The criterion must be named rather than presented as a universal optimum.

Time Series Components

Additive decomposition

\[ y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t. \]

Appropriate when seasonal amplitude and remainder variation are roughly stable across the level of the series.

T is trend-cycle, S seasonality, and R remainder. The equality is a structural representation; estimated components are denoted with hats.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/03-decomposition.html

Multiplicative decomposition

\[ y_t = T_t\times S_t\times R_t. \]

Appropriate when seasonal or remainder variation is approximately proportional to the series level.

Taking logs converts multiplication into addition:

\[ \log y_t = \log T_t + \log S_t + \log R_t. \]

Multiplicative components are ratios or indices, not additive quantities in the original unit.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/03-decomposition.html

Additive or multiplicative? Look at amplitude

idx = pd.date_range("2010-01-01", periods=120, freq="MS")
t = np.arange(120)
additive = 100 + .3*t + 10*np.sin(2*np.pi*t/12)
multiplicative = (100 + .3*t) * (1 + .12*np.sin(2*np.pi*t/12))
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2)
axes[0].plot(idx, additive); axes[0].set_title("Constant seasonal amplitude")
axes[1].plot(idx, multiplicative); axes[1].set_title("Seasonal amplitude grows with level")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

Seasonally adjusted series

For an additive decomposition:

\[ y_t^{\mathrm{SA}} = y_t-\widehat S_t =\widehat T_t+\widehat R_t. \]

For a multiplicative decomposition:

\[ y_t^{\mathrm{SA}}=\frac{y_t}{\widehat S_t}. \]

Seasonally adjusted does not mean smooth because the remainder remains. For turning points, the trend-cycle is usually the clearer visual object.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/03-decomposition.html

Seasonally adjusted versus trend-cycle

dcmp["seasonally_adjusted"] = dcmp["data"] - dcmp["seasonal"]

        label="Seasonally adjusted", linewidth=1)
ax.plot(dcmp["ds"], dcmp["trend"], color=COLORS["trend"],

ax.set(title="Seasonally adjusted series is not the trend", xlabel="Month", ylabel="Persons (thousands)")
ax.legend(frameon=False)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

3.3 Moving Averages

Centered moving average of odd order

For \(m=2k+1\):

\[ \widehat T_t = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=-k}^{k}y_{t+j}. \]

Nearby observations are averaged to suppress short-run fluctuations.

Odd order produces a symmetric centered window. Estimates are unavailable near endpoints without additional rules.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/03-decomposition.html

Equivalent weighted average

The \(2\times4\)-MA is

\[ \widehat T_t = \frac18y_{t-2} + \frac14y_{t-1} + \frac14y_t + \frac14y_{t+1} + \frac18y_{t+2}. \]

The weights are symmetric and sum to one.

Combining simple moving averages creates a weighted moving average with smaller weights at the endpoints.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/03-decomposition.html

Trend extraction should match seasonal period

  • quarterly, \(m=4\): use a centered \(2\times4\)-MA;
  • monthly, \(m=12\): use a centered \(2\times12\)-MA;
  • daily with weekly seasonality, \(m=7\): use a 7-MA.

A mismatched window can leave seasonal oscillation in the trend estimate.

The window is not selected only for visual smoothness. It is aligned with the seasonal period to average out the seasonal positions.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/03-decomposition.html

3.4 Classical Decomposition

Classical additive algorithm

For seasonal period \(m\):

  1. estimate \(\widehat T_t\) with an \(m\)-MA or centered \(2\times m\)-MA;
  2. detrend: \(d_t=y_t-\widehat T_t\);
  3. average \(d_t\) by seasonal position and normalize so seasonal factors sum to zero;
  4. calculate

\[ \widehat R_t=y_t-\widehat T_t-\widehat S_t. \]

The seasonal pattern is assumed constant from cycle to cycle.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/03-decomposition.html

Classical additive decomposition in Python

classical_add = seasonal_decompose(
    us_retail_employment.set_index("ds")["y"],
    model="additive", period=12, extrapolate_trend=0,
)
classical_frame = pd.DataFrame({
    "ds": us_retail_employment["ds"].to_numpy(),
    "data": us_retail_employment["y"].to_numpy(),
    "trend": classical_add.trend.to_numpy(),
    "seasonal": classical_add.seasonal.to_numpy(),
    "remainder": classical_add.resid.to_numpy(),
})

The statsmodels function operationalizes classical decomposition. Missing trend and remainder values at the endpoints are retained to make the limitation visible.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/03-decomposition.html

Classical multiplicative algorithm

  1. estimate \(\widehat T_t\);
  2. detrend: \(d_t=y_t/\widehat T_t\);
  3. average ratios by season and normalize so seasonal indices sum to \(m\);
  4. calculate

\[ \widehat R_t = \frac{y_t}{\widehat T_t\widehat S_t}. \]

Multiplicative seasonal indices average to one, equivalently sum to m across one full seasonal cycle.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/03-decomposition.html

Interpret the seasonal and remainder panels as ratios around one, not quantities in the original gas unit.

Limitations of classical decomposition

  • no trend-cycle or remainder estimates near endpoints;
  • rapid rises and falls can be over-smoothed;
  • seasonality is forced to repeat exactly;
  • changing seasonal patterns cannot be represented;
  • unusual observations can distort components.

The chapter explicitly does not recommend classical decomposition when better methods are available, although it remains useful for understanding the logic.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/03-decomposition.html

3.5 Official Seasonal Adjustment

X-11 and SEATS

Official statistical agencies commonly use variants of:

  • X-11, based on classical decomposition with additional procedures;
  • SEATS, Seasonal Extraction in ARIMA Time Series;
  • combinations of both.

They are designed primarily for monthly and quarterly official series.

The chapter states that these procedures are not intended for daily, hourly, or weekly seasonality.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/03-decomposition.html

What X-11 adds

The chapter highlights that X-11:

  • provides endpoint trend-cycle estimates;
  • allows slowly changing seasonality;
  • handles trading-day and holiday variation;
  • can include known predictors;
  • supports additive and multiplicative forms;
  • is designed to be robust to outliers and level shifts.

This slide summarizes capabilities explicitly described in the chapter. The detailed X-11 algorithm is beyond this lecture.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/03-decomposition.html

STL Decomposition

What is STL?

STL means Seasonal and Trend decomposition using Loess.

STL estimates:

  • a smooth trend-cycle;
  • a seasonal component that may evolve;
  • a remainder;
  • optionally robust weights for unusual observations.

LOESS is a local regression smoother. STL was introduced by Cleveland and coauthors and later extended to multiple seasonal patterns through MSTL.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/03-decomposition.html

Advantages and limitations

Advantages

  • supports many seasonal periods, not only monthly or quarterly;
  • seasonal evolution is controllable;
  • trend smoothness is controllable;
  • robust fitting can protect trend and seasonality from outliers.

Limitations

  • does not automatically handle trading-day or calendar effects;
  • directly provides additive decomposition only.

A multiplicative interpretation can be approached by logging positive data before STL.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/03-decomposition.html

Robust STL

stl_robust = STL(
    us_retail_employment["y"],
    period=12,
    seasonal=13,
    trend=21,
    robust=True,

Component scales can mislead

A decomposition plot usually gives each panel its own vertical scale.

Therefore:

  • panel height is not component importance;
  • compare axis values, not visual amplitude alone;
  • annotate units and zero lines;
  • use overlays when direct magnitude comparison matters.

This visual-literacy point is central to decomposition plots and appears again in the chapter exercises.

Overlay components on a common scale

Integrated Visual Workflow

Decomposition decision guide

Evidence Useful response
trading-day variation calendar adjustment
total driven by population per-capita adjustment
nominal monetary growth inflation adjustment
amplitude grows with level log or Box-Cox, then additive method
stable fixed seasonality classical decomposition may describe it
evolving seasonality STL
unusual observations robust STL and explicit investigation
multiple seasonal periods MSTL or another multi-seasonal method

This guide summarizes analytical choices. It is not a substitute for inspection and domain knowledge.

Visual diagnostic protocol

  1. plot the original series;
  2. label units and frequency;
  3. inspect seasonal amplitude against level;
  4. remove known calendar, population, or price effects;
  5. compare plausible transformations;
  6. estimate components;
  7. compare observed, trend, and adjusted series;
  8. inspect remainder over time and by ACF;
  9. test sensitivity to windows and robust fitting;
  10. document choices and limitations.

The protocol is the reusable output of the lecture.

What decomposition does not prove

Decomposition alone does not prove:

  • causality;
  • that a remainder is random;
  • that a trend will continue;
  • that a seasonal pattern is structurally stable;
  • that the selected transformation is optimal;
  • that forecasts will be accurate.

Components are estimated descriptive structures, not autonomous causal mechanisms.

Key Takeaways

Main conclusions

  1. Adjust known measurement effects before decomposition.
  2. Use transformations when variation changes with the level.
  3. Additive and multiplicative decompositions imply different units.
  4. Moving averages reveal trend but create endpoint and smoothing trade-offs.
  5. Classical decomposition assumes fixed seasonality and is not robust.
  6. Seasonally adjusted data still contain the remainder.
  7. STL allows controllable trend and evolving seasonality.
  8. Robust STL protects components, not the observed outlier itself.
  9. Component plots require careful attention to vertical scales.
  10. Decomposition is a hypothesis-producing visual analysis, not causal proof.

Ask students to distinguish trend-cycle from seasonally adjusted data before ending the lecture.

Introduction

Real time-series data are messy

A recorded series may contain:

  • observations that are unusually large or small;
  • values that are absent;
  • zeros whose meaning is ambiguous;
  • data-entry or sensor failures;
  • genuine shocks and structural changes.

The first task is not automatic correction. It is diagnosis.

The source section states that real data often contain missing values, outlying observations, and other messy features. This lecture focuses only on that subsection.

Outliers

What is an outlier?

An outlier is an observation that is very different from most observations in the time series.

An outlier may be:

  • a recording or processing error;
  • a valid but unusual event;
  • evidence of model misspecification;
  • evidence of a level shift or structural change.

The chapter explicitly states that outliers may be errors or may simply be unusual. The additional classifications are teaching categories for investigation.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/13-practical.html#sec-missing-outliers

Why time context matters

A value can appear ordinary marginally but be unusual for:

  • its season;
  • its neighboring observations;
  • the current trend level;
  • its day of week or quarter;
  • the variance regime at that time.

Outlier detection for time series should respect temporal structure.

This is a teaching recommendation. A global boxplot of the raw data may flag regular seasonal peaks rather than anomalies.

IQR rule on the remainder

Let

\operatorname{IQR}=Q_3-Q_1.

A general fence with multiplier \(k\) is

L=Q_1-k\operatorname{IQR},
\qquad
U=Q_3+k\operatorname{IQR}.

Flag observations for which \(R_t<L\) or \(R_t>U\).

The book discusses k equal to 1.5 and prefers a stricter k equal to 3 for this context.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/13-practical.html#sec-missing-outliers

Implement the chapter’s 3-IQR rule

q1 = dcmp["remainder"].quantile(.25)
q3 = dcmp["remainder"].quantile(.75)
iqr = q3 - q1
lower = q1 - 3 * iqr
upper = q3 + 3 * iqr
outliers = dcmp.loc[~dcmp["remainder"].between(lower, upper)].copy()

A rule is not a diagnosis

Before replacing a flag, ask:

  • Is the raw source correct?
  • Did units or definitions change?
  • Was there a one-off real event?
  • Is the seasonal period correctly specified?
  • Did the decomposition leave systematic structure?
  • Is the value part of a level shift rather than an isolated outlier?

These are recommended diagnostic questions. The source warns against replacement without understanding why the observation occurred.

Missing Values

Missing is not a value

A missing observation may mean:

  • the event was not recorded;
  • the device failed;
  • the reporting process was delayed;
  • the activity did not occur;
  • the business was closed;
  • the record is structurally inapplicable.

These meanings require different treatments.

The source distinguishes context-driven missingness, such as public-holiday closures, from essentially random recording failures.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/13-practical.html#sec-missing-outliers

Zero versus missing

0       = observed absence or true zero quantity
NaN     = no usable observation is recorded

Never convert NaN to zero unless zero accurately represents the data-generating process.

The source gives a holiday-closure example where setting a missing value to zero may be reasonable when closure means zero sales, but the model must also account for holiday and post-holiday effects.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/13-practical.html#sec-missing-outliers

Missingness can be informative

The source example:

  • a store is closed on a public holiday;
  • sales are zero on the holiday;
  • sales may increase on the following day;
  • ignoring the mechanism can bias forecasts around the holiday.

No automated method can infer this business context on its own.

The chapter suggests setting holiday closure values to zero and using dummy variables for the holiday and the following day in an AutoARIMA model.

[Sources] - https://otexts.com/fpppy/13-practical.html#sec-missing-outliers

Imputation

Interpolation is an estimate

Linear interpolation between observed endpoints \(y_a\) and \(y_b\) is

\widehat y_t
=
y_a+
\frac{t-a}{b-a}(y_b-y_a),
\qquad a<t<b.

It assumes smooth movement between neighboring observations.

Interpolation creates estimated values, not recovered truths. Long gaps and seasonal series make the assumption less plausible.

Reproduce the source’s interpolation approach

Do not overwrite the raw data

Keep at least:

  • original value;
  • cleaned or imputed value;
  • modification flag;
  • reason for modification;
  • method used;
  • parameters or window;
  • analyst review status.

This is a recommended governance pattern. It supports reproducibility and auditability.

Visual honesty checklist

  • show gaps rather than silently connecting them;
  • use a distinct marker for imputed values;
  • retain the original series for comparison;
  • disclose the detection rule;
  • disclose the replacement method;
  • avoid calling flags confirmed errors;
  • report how many observations were modified;
  • inspect whether cleaning changes substantive conclusions.

Outlier decision tree

  NO

The source says replacement is more defensible if the value is genuinely erroneous or if the event will not occur during the forecast period.

Missing-value decision tree

MISSING OBSERVATION
        ↓
WHAT DOES MISSING MEAN?
  ├─ TRUE ZERO / CLOSURE → ENCODE ZERO + EVENT VARIABLES
  ├─ RANDOM RECORDING FAILURE → ESTIMATE IF NEEDED
  ├─ STRUCTURAL NON-APPLICABILITY → RETAIN AS MISSING
  └─ UNKNOWN → INVESTIGATE BEFORE IMPUTING

The source emphasizes that treatment depends on forecasting context and that automated methods cannot infer contextual effects such as public holidays.

Practice

Classroom exercise

Using the Adelaide Hills series:

  1. plot the raw series and annotate 2002 Q4;
  2. perform robust STL with period 4;
  3. calculate 1.5-IQR and 3-IQR remainder fences;
  4. compare the flagged sets;
  5. investigate whether flags form isolated points or clusters;
  6. replace only one selected candidate by interpolation;
  7. compare trend and remainder before and after;
  8. create an audit table;
  9. explain why the chosen treatment is defensible;
  10. state what evidence remains unavailable.

Assessment should reward reasoning, transparency, and visual comparison rather than maximizing the number of detected outliers.

Missingness experiment

Create three versions of the series:

  • one isolated missing quarter;
  • four consecutive missing quarters;
  • one missing seasonal peak every year.

For each version:

  • visualize the pattern of missingness;
  • compare linear, forward-fill, and seasonal-lag imputation;
  • explain which strategy is least implausible;
  • identify possible forecast bias.

This extension illustrates that the temporal mechanism and gap pattern matter more than a universal imputation rule.

Assessment rubric

Dimension Evidence
Temporal reasoning seasonality and trend are removed before flagging
Visualization candidates, gaps, and replacements are visible
Context error, event, zero, and missing are distinguished
Method detection and replacement procedures are explicit
Sensitivity alternative thresholds or imputations are compared
Transparency original values and audit flags are retained
Interpretation cleaning consequences are assessed, not hidden

A strong submission treats cleaning as a model choice with consequences.

Key Takeaways

Main conclusions

  1. An outlier can be an error or a genuine event.
  2. Time-series outliers should be judged relative to temporal structure.
  3. Robust STL can isolate unusual values in the remainder.
  4. IQR fences produce candidates, not confirmed errors.
  5. Missing values and zeros are not interchangeable.
  6. Missingness mechanisms can affect forecast bias.
  7. Interpolation is an assumption-based estimate.
  8. Different imputations can produce different stories.
  9. Cleaning can alter trends, seasonality, remainders, and forecasts.
  10. Every modification should remain visible and auditable.

End by asking students whether a smoother series is necessarily a more truthful series. The correct answer is no.
