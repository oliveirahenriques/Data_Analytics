Introduction

Missing data are part of the data-generating process

Missing observations may result from:

  • power outages;
  • disconnected or defective sensors;
  • reporting delays;
  • survey non-response;
  • privacy or compliance rules;
  • source-system placeholders;
  • irregular collection schedules.

Chapter 6 emphasizes that missingness is inevitable in real-world data and that domain context is essential for deciding how to handle it.

[Sources] - Chapter 6, Handling Missing Data, pages 185–188, attached book.

Learning objectives

Students will be able to:

  • identify missing observations and placeholders;
  • visualize missingness through time;
  • distinguish isolated gaps from contiguous outages;
  • compare deletion, tagging, imputation, and interpolation;
  • apply pandas and scikit-learn imputers;
  • evaluate imputations using masked ground truth;
  • combine RMSE and visual diagnostics;
  • prevent leakage in forecasting workflows.

The chapter follows a structured process: ingest, identify, impute, evaluate against original data, then visualize and compare methods.

[Sources] - Chapter 6, page 187, attached book.

Why visualization is essential

A scalar missing-value count does not reveal:

  • where gaps occur;
  • whether gaps form blocks;
  • whether missingness coincides with seasonality;
  • whether one variable is missing whenever another changes;
  • whether imputation smooths away meaningful variability.

The chapter combines RMSE with visualization because a lower error does not necessarily preserve the randomness and variability of the original data.

[Sources] - Chapter 6, pages 189 and 220–221, attached book.

Understanding Missing Data

Python representations

Common missing representations include:

  • np.nan for numeric values;
  • pd.NA for nullable pandas types;
  • NaT for missing datetimes;
  • blank cells;
  • source-system placeholders such as 99999, 0, ?, or N/A.

Chapter 6 notes that pandas typically represents missing observations with NaN, NaT, or NA and warns that placeholders must be investigated carefully.

[Sources] - Chapter 6, pages 189–190, attached book.

Missing is not automatically zero

0       = an observed value that may be valid
NaN     = no usable numeric observation
NaT     = no usable datetime observation

A zero should only be converted to missing after validating the domain meaning.

The chapter specifically warns that zero may be either a legitimate value or a placeholder and should not be replaced automatically.

[Sources] - Chapter 6, page 189, attached book.

Missingness mechanisms

A useful statistical vocabulary is:

  • MCAR: missingness unrelated to observed or unobserved values;
  • MAR: missingness related to observed variables;
  • MNAR: missingness related to the missing value itself.

These mechanisms are assumptions, not labels that can be established from a heatmap alone.

This slide adds standard missing-data terminology for teaching. The chapter motivates the importance of causes, censoring, and bias but does not provide this three-part taxonomy in the retrieved text.

Deduction: missingness can carry information

Instead of erasing missingness, preserve it with:

  • an indicator column;
  • a cause label;
  • source-system metadata;
  • outage duration;
  • collection-status flags.

Chapter 6 recommends tagging missing data when the cause is known and shows SimpleImputer with add_indicator=True. Zero indicates observed and one indicates originally missing.

[Sources] - Chapter 6, pages 188 and 214–215, attached book.

Strategy Before Imputation

Four broad responses

  1. Drop observations or variables.
  2. Tag missingness and preserve context.
  3. Impute using statistical or machine-learning methods.
  4. Interpolate using temporal continuity.

The chapter discusses dropping, tagging, and estimating missing values, and devotes a separate recipe to interpolation.

[Sources] - Chapter 6, pages 188–189 and 223 onward, attached book.

Dropping can erase the missingness pattern

Dropping is especially risky when:

  • data collection is costly;
  • the sample is small;
  • missingness is clustered;
  • missingness is related to the target;
  • the gap itself is operationally meaningful.

Chapter 6 warns that premature deletion can lose information and obscure whether missingness reflects censoring or bias.

[Sources] - Chapter 6, page 188, attached book.

Controlled Evaluation

Why create artificial gaps?

For naturally missing observations, the true values are unknown.

A controlled evaluation:

  1. starts from observed values;
  2. hides selected observations;
  3. imputes the hidden values;
  4. compares estimates with retained ground truth.

Chapter 6 intentionally removes values while retaining the original data so imputation methods can be evaluated against ground truth.

RMSE is not enough

A method can obtain a low RMSE while:

  • oversmoothing the series;
  • suppressing random variability;
  • distorting seasonal peaks;
  • creating implausible local dynamics;
  • leaking future information.

The chapter explicitly warns that lower RMSE does not necessarily mean better imputation because it may overlook randomness and variability. RMSE should be combined with visualization.

When carry-forward may be reasonable

Forward fill is more plausible when:

  • the process changes slowly;
  • the last state persists until a new measurement;
  • the gap is short;
  • future information is unavailable;
  • stepwise behavior is expected.

It is less plausible for strong trends or long gaps.

These are methodological recommendations for interpreting forward fill. They are not universal rules.

Package-based univariate imputation

[Sources] - Chapter 6, pages 208–213, attached book.

Fit on training data only

Correct forecasting workflow:

imputer.fit(train[["value"]])
train_imputed = imputer.transform(train[["value"]])
test_imputed = imputer.transform(test[["value"]])

Incorrect workflow:

imputer.fit(full_dataset[["value"]])

The chapter explains that fitting calculates statistics from the supplied data and notes that fitting is usually done on the training dataset.

[Sources] - Chapter 6, page 212, attached book.

SimpleImputer parameters

  • missing_values: representation to replace;
  • strategy: mean, median, most_frequent, or constant;
  • fill_value: value used for constant replacement;
  • add_indicator: append missingness flags.

These parameters are described in the chapter’s SimpleImputer discussion.

Interpolation

Interpolation uses temporal position

Linear interpolation between known endpoints is

\widehat x_t=x_a+\frac{t-a}{b-a}(x_b-x_a).

Unlike global mean imputation, interpolation uses neighboring values and temporal spacing.

The chapter’s interpolation recipe compares linear and higher-order interpolation methods and recommends limiting how many consecutive values are filled.

Multivariate Imputation

Why use other variables?

Univariate imputation uses the target variable in isolation.

Multivariate imputation exploits relationships among variables to estimate missing values.

Possible benefits are strongest when features are meaningfully related.

Chapter 6 contrasts univariate imputation with multivariate methods that use relationships among variables and introduces IterativeImputer and KNNImputer.

Visualization and Decision Principles

Show observed and imputed values differently

Use distinct visual encodings:

  • solid line for observed values;
  • dashed line for imputed spans;
  • markers for imputed points;
  • shaded region for missing blocks;
  • an indicator panel underneath the series.

This is a visualization recommendation aligned with the chapter’s repeated visual comparison of original, missing, and imputed series.

Do not overwrite the raw observations

Maintain:

  • original value;
  • incomplete value;
  • imputed value;
  • missing indicator;
  • method name;
  • method parameters;
  • reason for missingness;
  • review status.

The chapter preserves original datasets for evaluation and demonstrates missing indicators. The additional audit fields are recommended for reproducibility.

Avoid look-ahead leakage

For forecasting:

  • fit imputers on training data only;
  • do not use future observations for real-time imputation;
  • distinguish retrospective cleaning from online availability;
  • reproduce the information set available at each forecast origin.

The chapter notes that fitting an imputer is usually performed on the training dataset. The remaining points are forecasting-specific recommendations.

Common Errors

Missing-data failure modes

  • converting every zero to NaN;
  • dropping rows before studying missingness patterns;
  • using global means on trending series;
  • forward filling across long outages;
  • using spline interpolation without checking overshoot;
  • fitting imputers on the full dataset before splitting;
  • evaluating naturally missing values without ground truth;
  • selecting a method by RMSE alone;
  • hiding imputed values in the final chart;
  • treating imputed values as equally certain as observations.

These cautions combine the chapter’s warnings about placeholders, deletion, RMSE limitations, and training-data fitting with visualization best practices.

Key Takeaways

Main conclusions

  1. Missingness has location, duration, cause, and information content.
  2. A missing timestamp differs from a NaN in an existing row.
  3. Placeholders require domain validation.
  4. Deletion can remove informative patterns.
  5. Univariate methods are simple but ignore other variables.
  6. Interpolation uses temporal continuity but becomes uncertain over long gaps.
  7. Multivariate methods can exploit relationships among features.
  8. RMSE should be calculated on deliberately masked ground truth.
  9. Lower RMSE does not guarantee realistic variability.
  10. Imputed values should remain visible, flagged, and auditable.

These conclusions synthesize the chapter’s diagnosis, pandas and scikit-learn imputation, multivariate methods, interpolation, and evaluation guidance.
